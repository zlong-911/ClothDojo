\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Garment manipulation policies}

Many methods define a set of task-specific primitives for garment flattening
and folding. A policy selects a primitive or predicts parameters such as grasp
locations from visual observations, while a predefined controller executes
the continuous motion~\cite{flingbot,clothmate,clothfunnels,speedfolding,unifolding}.
The policy can observe the cloth again between primitives, and some primitives
also use feedback during operations such as stretching. These action priors
reduce the learning space and have been used for flattening multiple garment
types and for structured folding.

Another approach learns continuous actions from data. Recent VLAs directly
predict action chunks and perform long sequences of picking up, flattening,
folding, and stacking garments on real robots~\cite{pistar06,chi0,demavla}.
These systems establish initial behavior with human demonstrations, expose
failures through autonomous rollouts, and use human corrections to cover
states encountered by the policy. This learning cycle also lets the policy
learn action ordering, bimanual coordination, and adjustments during execution.

\subsection{Simulation environments and data generation}

Existing garment simulation platforms provide diverse garment assets and
dynamic robot--cloth interaction for tasks including flattening and
folding~\cite{garmentlab,dexgarmentlab,lehome,simweaver}. Their publicly
described trajectories primarily come from task primitives, demonstration
transfer, or limited teleoperation.

Automatic trajectory expansion largely follows two approaches. Programmatic
methods construct trajectories using keypoints, task stages, or folding
patterns~\cite{foldnet,instantfold}. Demonstration transfer adapts segmented
behaviors to new assets and initial states, then executes them to obtain new
physical trajectories~\cite{softmimicgen,sim1}. The former supports batched
execution of specified task procedures, while the latter reuses existing
demonstrations; both have effectively expanded simulation datasets. Existing
large-scale results mainly concern folding tasks with well-defined steps.
For severely crumpled garments, however, the course of flattening changes with
the cloth state, making fixed procedures and segmented demonstrations
difficult to reuse directly.

ClothDojo brings the demonstration--rollout--correction cycle used in
real-robot garment manipulation to simulation trajectory generation. The
trajectory agent learns from human interaction and then runs autonomously on
diverse garments and complex initial states; Fig.~\ref{fig:folding-trajectories}
shows examples of the resulting behavior.
