\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Metrics and success criteria}
\label{sec:success-criteria}

We compute task metrics at every simulation step and require them to remain
within their success thresholds, preventing a transient pass through the goal
from being counted as success.

\textbf{Flattening.} We align the current garment with a naturally laid-flat
reference of the same garment using an in-plane translation and rotation. The
mean vertex distance after alignment defines the deformable error
$d_{\mathrm{def}}$; the difference between the estimated orientation and the
nearest accepted orientation defines the rotation error $d_{\mathrm{rot}}$.
Both errors are normalized by the planar scale of the reference garment. We
estimate the upper bounds of valid flattening outcomes from 890 expert terminal
states across 182 garments, obtaining thresholds of 0.088 and 0.043. A rollout
succeeds when both errors remain below their respective thresholds for 1 s.

\textbf{Folding.} Because the task admits multiple valid outcomes, we do not
match the result to a single template. Instead, the fold-quality score $Q$
combines area compression, overall flatness, top-surface rectangularity,
three-dimensional material overlap, and proximity to the table. We fit the
score using expert terminal states, successful HIL states, and failed rollouts,
and calibrate the threshold with garment-level held-out splits. A rollout
succeeds when $Q\geq0.142$ for 3 s.

\subsection{How well does the trajectory agent generate data?}
\label{sec:trajectory-agent-eval}

We test whether NOCS, mirror augmentation, and HIL progressively improve the
agent's ability to generate successful trajectories. Each agent first learns
from expert demonstrations; HIL corrections then cover states encountered
during autonomous execution. Table~\ref{tab:human-training-data} summarizes the
two sources of human data and their garment coverage. Folding demonstrations
start from the terminal states of their corresponding flattening
demonstrations. Because these states are relatively controlled, we collect
fewer repeated demonstrations per garment and cover more garments instead.
All trajectories are cleaned before training; the table also reports their
sizes after the mirror augmentation in Sec.~\ref{sec:experience-corrections}.

\subsection{What is the value of automatically generated data?}

We initialize all policies from the same pretrained $\pi_{0.5}$ weights and
train separate RGB policies for flattening and folding using three data
sources. \textbf{Human} contains human-collected trajectories,
\textbf{Generated} contains automatically generated trajectories that pass the
success criteria, and \textbf{Combined} is their union.
Table~\ref{tab:rgb-policy-training-data} summarizes the number of trajectories
and garments in each training set.

  \caption{RGB-policy training data (trajectories / garments).}

  \footnotesize

    Task & Human & Generated & Combined \\

    Fold & 748 / 242 & 3,856 / 399 & 4,604 / 458 \\

We evaluate garment and appearance generalization on a common test set. The
Seen split contains 100 garments represented in all three training sets, while
the Unseen split contains another 100 garments absent from all three. Each
garment has four fixed held-out initial states, evaluated under both Seen and
Unseen appearances. Seen appearances are sampled from the training
randomization distributions; Unseen appearances use garment textures, support
surfaces, backgrounds, and lighting conditions withheld from training. All
policies are evaluated on the same garments, initial states, and appearances.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/rgb-policy-by-metric.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Generated trajectories train effective RGB policies.}
  Success rates are reported on common sets of Seen and Unseen garments, each
  evaluated with Seen and Unseen appearances.}
  \label{fig:generated-data-value}

Generated outperforms Human under every evaluation condition in
Fig.~\ref{fig:generated-data-value}. Although generated trajectories have less
refined terminal states than human trajectories, they span more trajectories
and garments. These results suggest that, for the RGB policies studied here,
broader manipulation coverage may be more valuable than finer terminal states.

Simply combining human and generated trajectories provides no further gain.
Combined has lower aggregate success than Human on flattening; on folding, it
outperforms Human but remains below Generated overall. Despite containing the
most data, Combined is not the best-performing training set, indicating that
the distribution and mixture of data sources affect policy learning.

Unseen garments cause a larger performance drop than Unseen appearances. This
trend holds across all three training sets and both tasks, suggesting that
generalization across garment structures remains the larger challenge and
motivating broader garment coverage in the training data.

\subsection{Where do current policies fail?}

Aggregate success mixes several causes of failure. Conditioning results on
initial distance and garment morphology exposes geometric difficulty. Holding
the physical case fixed while changing texture, background, or lighting exposes
visual sensitivity. Changing validated physical parameters exposes dynamics
sensitivity. Together these views turn the task into a diagnostic benchmark
rather than a single leaderboard number.

\subsection{Do memory and action models help?}

Flattening creates reasons for temporal reasoning that are absent from a
single-frame task. Earlier views can reveal garment identity before it becomes
occluded, and the result of a failed dynamic action indicates how the next
action should change. History, explicit memory, action chunking, RTC, and
world-action models represent different ways to use this information. Their
value is determined by where they improve recovery and difficult cases, not by
their average score alone.

\subsection{Dynamics and real-world transfer}

Dynamic actions couple cloth properties with robot execution. Changes in
friction, stiffness, damping, tracking bandwidth, or action latency can alter
the result of an otherwise identical command. Robustness experiments therefore
test whether a policy observes and corrects physical outcomes instead of
replaying a fixed motion. Real-data experiments hold human data fixed so that
any gain can be attributed to the additional simulated trajectories.
