\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Garment flattening and folding require visual understanding and bimanual
control throughout long sequences of interaction. Garments differ in structure
and appearance. Wrinkles and self-occlusion hide relevant parts, and each
contact changes the cloth configuration and the conditions for subsequent
actions. Policies must continually adjust to the outcomes of their actions.
Training across garments therefore requires continuous trajectories covering
many initial states. Collecting these trajectories on real robots is
time-consuming and requires repeatedly setting up complex configurations that
are difficult to reproduce. Differences in robots and scenes further limit
the reuse of data and experiments, making training and evaluation at scale
costly.

Simulation reduces interaction costs by allowing initial states to be reused
and rollouts to run in batches, but successful trajectories still need to be
designed or collected. Task-specific primitives, keypoint-based planning, and
prescribed steps can generate trajectories automatically, but require the
motion patterns or task structure to be designed in advance. Human
teleoperation allows adjustments during execution, yet remains limited by the
cost of collecting each trajectory. Fig.~\ref{fig:folding-trajectories} shows
midair regrasping in autonomous flattening rollouts, and hand switching and
post-fold adjustments in folding rollouts. We study how to generate
trajectories that include these behaviors at scale.

Real-robot laundry systems show that demonstrations, human corrections, and
autonomous experience can form an effective data collection and learning
cycle~\cite{pistar06,chi0,demavla}. Inspired by these systems, ClothDojo uses a
closed-loop policy as a trajectory agent to generate simulation data from new
states. We first train the agent on human demonstrations using supervised
fine-tuning (SFT). The agent then performs batched rollouts: task metrics automatically
select successful trajectories, failed rollouts receive human-in-the-loop
(HIL) corrections, and these corrections are added to the training data for
the next round. Simulation also allows normalized object coordinate space
(NOCS) to be encoded as cloth
appearance. Through the standard image interface of a vision-language-action
model (VLA), these coordinates provide a canonical position reference for
learning cloth interaction. Human effort is thus concentrated on
demonstrations and necessary corrections, while the agent expands the
trajectory collection through autonomous rollouts.

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}, ClothDojo connects learned trajectory
generation, data expansion, and closed-loop evaluation through dynamic
interaction between bimanual robots and cloth. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}[\setlength{\labelindent}{0pt}]
  \item \textbf{A data generation and learning cycle.} A shared dynamic
  robot--cloth environment combines human demonstrations and corrections,
  batched rollouts, and automatic success filtering, turning adaptive human
  manipulation into a scalable source of trajectories.
  \item \textbf{A trajectory agent with privileged visual observations.}
  NOCS provides canonical position cues through the VLA's standard image
  interface. Trained with SFT and HIL corrections, the agent directly predicts
  continuous bimanual actions without task-specific primitives or prescribed
  steps.
  \item \textbf{A large trajectory dataset and closed-loop evaluation.}
  To our knowledge, we construct the first dataset of successful flattening
  and folding trajectories generated autonomously by learned continuous
  control policies across hundreds of garments: 500 garments, with ten
  trajectories per garment for each task, totaling 10,000 trajectories.
  Successful trajectories are re-rendered with randomized appearances for
  training policies on ordinary RGB observations. A fixed protocol evaluates
  flattening, folding, and their sequential execution.
\end{enumerate}
